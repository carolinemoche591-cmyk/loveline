A STORY OF DUTY, LOVE & SACRIFICE

When the Guns
Fall Silent

He was trained to survive every battlefield. She was the one thing he had never learned how to lose.

A MILITARY ROMANCE NOVEL

Chapter One

The Soldier Who Came Home

"Some men return from war with medals on their chests. Others return with ghosts in their eyes."

The first thing Captain Adrian Blackwood noticed when he stepped off the military transport was the rain.

It fell in thin silver lines across the airfield, gathering on the shoulders of his uniform and sliding down the side of his face. Somewhere behind him, soldiers shouted instructions. Engines roared. Boots struck wet concrete. The familiar machinery of military life continued around him as if nothing in the world had changed.

But everything had changed.

Adrian had spent eleven months deployed in a conflict zone where the nights were filled with distant explosions and the mornings often began with names being crossed off a list. He had learned to recognize the difference between ordinary silence and the kind that came immediately before an attack. He had learned to sleep with one hand close to his weapon. He had learned to keep his face expressionless when another man was carried away beneath a bloodstained blanket.

What he had not learned was how to return home.

At thirty-two, Adrian was one of the youngest officers in his unit to have received the Silver Cross for exceptional courage under fire. His commanding officers called him dependable. His men called him unbreakable. The newspapers had once described him as a decorated hero after a rescue operation that had saved seventeen soldiers trapped behind enemy lines.

Adrian hated that word.

Heroes, in his experience, were usually men who had survived long enough for other people to tell their stories. They were men who returned home carrying the names of those who had not. They were men who smiled for photographs while remembering the sound of someone begging for help in the dark.

He had not told anyone that he still heard Daniel's voice sometimes.

"Blackwood!"

Adrian turned.

Sergeant Marcus Reed was standing beside a military vehicle, his broad face split by the kind of grin that had somehow survived years of deployment.

"You planning to stand there until next Christmas?" Marcus called. "Your ride is waiting."

Adrian glanced at the black SUV parked near the terminal.

"I thought I was getting a taxi."

"And miss the privilege of driving you home myself? Never." Marcus opened the passenger door. "Besides, I promised your mother I'd deliver you in one piece."

"You promised my mother?"

"She called me three times."

Adrian shook his head and climbed into the vehicle.

"Traitor."

Marcus laughed as he slid behind the steering wheel. "That's Sergeant Traitor to you, Captain."

For a few minutes, the familiar teasing made Adrian feel almost normal. They drove past the airfield gates and onto the main road, where traffic crawled beneath a grey sky. Shopkeepers lifted their shutters. Schoolchildren hurried beneath oversized umbrellas. A woman argued with a motorcycle rider at a junction.

Ordinary life.

It seemed impossibly fragile.

"You going to tell me what happens now?" Marcus asked.

"Now?"

"You've got thirty days of leave. Maybe more, depending on what headquarters decides. What are you going to do with yourself?"

"Sleep."

"For thirty days?"

"If necessary."

Marcus glanced at him. "You know most people take leave to see their families, go on holiday, find a woman."

"Most people aren't you."

"True. I'm handsome enough to have options."

Adrian almost smiled.

Almost.

Marcus noticed, and his expression softened. "Listen, brother. I'm serious. You don't have to spend every waking moment pretending you're fine."

Adrian watched the rain sliding across the window.

"I'm not pretending."

"Right. And I'm the president."

Neither man spoke for a while.

Eventually, Marcus turned onto a quieter road lined with jacaranda trees. Their purple blossoms lay scattered on the wet pavement like pieces of torn silk.

"There's one more thing," Marcus said.

Adrian already disliked the sound of it.

"What?"

"Your mother told me to remind you about the family dinner on Saturday."

"Absolutely not."

"I told her you'd say that."

"Then why mention it?"

"Because she said she'd come to your house and drag you there herself."

Adrian leaned back against the seat and closed his eyes.

For the first time since the aircraft had landed, he genuinely wished he had remained overseas.

* * *

Across the city, a young woman was arguing with a coffee machine.

"You have one job," Elena Grace muttered. "One. I put the cup underneath you, I press the button, and you give me coffee. This is not a complicated relationship."

The machine made an unpleasant grinding noise.

Elena stared at it.

"Don't you dare."

Brown liquid dribbled onto the counter.

Elena closed her eyes.

"I hate this place."

"You said that yesterday."

Elena turned to find her best friend, Sophie, standing behind her with two folders tucked beneath one arm.

"Yesterday, the printer ate my report," Elena replied. "Today, the coffee machine has declared war. At this rate, tomorrow the building will collapse on me."

Sophie considered this. "At least you won't have to attend the staff meeting."

Elena laughed despite herself.

At twenty-seven, she had perfected the art of appearing cheerful even when her life felt like a collection of unfinished tasks. She worked as a civilian medical coordinator at a military hospital, organizing patient transfers, assisting families, and making sure that wounded service members received the treatment they needed.

She had never worn a uniform. She had never carried a weapon. She had never stood in the middle of a battlefield wondering whether the next breath would be her last.

But she knew what war did to people.

She had seen young men arrive with injuries they would carry for the rest of their lives. She had watched mothers hold their sons' hands and wives sit beside hospital beds waiting for husbands who could no longer remember their names. She had listened to soldiers apologize for things that were never their fault.

And she had learned that the worst injuries were not always the ones that appeared on medical scans.

"By the way," Sophie said, setting down the folders, "guess who's back in the country."

Elena continued wiping coffee from the counter.

"Your ex?"

"First of all, rude. Second, no. Captain Adrian Blackwood."

Elena stopped wiping.

The name seemed to change the temperature of the room.

Sophie watched her carefully.

"You heard me."

Elena placed the cloth beside the machine.

"Good for him."

"That's all?"

"What else should I say?"

"Perhaps something along the lines of, 'Oh my goodness, Sophie, the man who disappeared from my life without a proper goodbye has finally returned.'"

Elena shot her a look.

"You have a very active imagination."

"And you have an extremely suspicious ability to pretend that you don't care about people who have clearly broken your heart."

"He didn't break my heart."

Sophie folded her arms.

Elena looked away first.

There had been a time when Adrian Blackwood occupied more space in her thoughts than anyone else. They had met four years earlier at a charity event organized to support injured service members and their families. He had been younger then, though no less serious, wearing his dress uniform as if it were an extension of his body.

She remembered the first thing he had said to her.

She had been carrying a tray of drinks when someone bumped into her. A glass tipped, splashing orange juice across the front of Adrian's immaculate uniform.

Elena had stared at the stain in horror.

"Oh, no. I'm so sorry."

He had looked down at his chest, then back at her.

"I was beginning to think this uniform was invincible."

She had blinked.

"You're joking?"

"Only a little."

Then he had smiled.

It was a small smile, brief and unexpected, but it had transformed his face. For the rest of the evening, she found herself looking for him across the room.

They had spoken again two weeks later. Then they had started meeting for coffee. Their conversations had become longer, their silences more comfortable, and their goodbyes more difficult.

Adrian was not a man who gave away his feelings easily. He rarely spoke about himself, and whenever Elena asked him about his future, he answered with the same careful honesty.

"I don't know what tomorrow looks like."

She had eventually learned that it was his way of saying he was afraid to promise her anything.

Still, there had been promises.

He had promised to call when he could. He had promised to return whenever his deployment allowed. Once, during a night when rain had trapped them beneath the awning outside her apartment, he had held her face between his hands and told her that she was the first person who had made him imagine a life beyond the military.

She had believed him.

Then his last deployment had begun.

At first, the messages came regularly. A short text in the morning. A photograph of a terrible meal. A voice message recorded when the connection was good enough to carry his voice across the distance.

Then the messages became shorter.

Then they stopped.

One month became two. Two became three. Elena sent messages she later regretted and deleted messages she wished she had sent. She called the number she had memorized and listened to it ring without an answer.

Finally, she received a brief message from a military contact explaining that Adrian was serving in a location where communications were restricted.

It was not an explanation. It was barely even a sentence.

But she waited.

She waited until waiting began to feel like a second job.

Then she stopped.

Not because she stopped loving him, but because she had finally understood that love could not be the only thing holding two people together.

"Elena."

Sophie's voice brought her back to the present.

"I'm fine," Elena said.

"I didn't ask."

"You were about to."

Sophie sighed. "He's back, Elena. That's all I'm saying. You don't have to see him. You don't have to speak to him. But don't pretend his name means nothing to you."

Elena picked up her coffee cup, discovered it was empty, and put it down again.

"I have patients to organize."

"Of course you do."

"And reports."

"Naturally."

"And I need to repair this coffee machine."

Sophie smiled.

"Tell the captain I said welcome home."

"There is no captain in my life."

But when Sophie walked away, Elena looked at her phone.

For one reckless second, she wondered whether Adrian still had her number.

* * *

Adrian's mother's house smelled of cinnamon, fresh bread, and the kind of warmth that made him feel guilty for having stayed away so long.

He stood on the front step with his travel bag in one hand and considered turning around.

The door opened before he could make up his mind.

"Adrian!"

His mother pulled him into an embrace so fierce that his bag slipped from his fingers.

Margaret Blackwood had always been a small woman with a formidable capacity for worry. Her hair had turned almost entirely silver over the past few years, but her eyes remained sharp enough to notice every unspoken thought passing across her son's face.

She held him at arm's length.

"Look at you."

"Hello, Mum."

"You're thinner."

"I'm not."

"Your face is thinner."

"My face is the same."

"And you look tired."

"I've been travelling."

She narrowed her eyes. "Don't argue with your mother when she's happy you're alive."

That silenced him.

Margaret's expression softened. She touched his cheek with the back of her fingers.

"Come inside, darling."

The house was almost exactly as he remembered it. The old clock still ticked in the hallway. Family photographs lined the walls. A framed picture of Adrian at seventeen, wearing his first military uniform, hung beside a more recent photograph of him standing beside his father.

His father had died six years earlier, before Adrian's career had taken him into its most dangerous chapters.

Sometimes Adrian wondered whether his father would have been proud of him.

Sometimes he feared he would have recognized how much of his son had disappeared.

"Sit," Margaret ordered. "I've made lunch."

"You didn't have to."

"I know."

She placed a bowl in front of him and watched while he took the first spoonful.

"Well?"

"It's good."

"Good? I spent three hours preparing it."

"It's excellent."

"Better."

For a little while, they spoke about harmless things. His aunt's upcoming visit. The neighbor who had decided to keep chickens in her backyard. The leak in the roof that his mother insisted she could repair herself.

Then Margaret put down her spoon.

"Have you spoken to Elena?"

Adrian's hand stopped halfway to his mouth.

His mother watched him quietly.

"I thought not."

He placed the spoon back in the bowl.

"Mum."

"I know it's none of my business."

"Then why bring it up?"

"Because I watched you come home from your last leave with a smile on your face that I hadn't seen since your father was alive. And I watched you leave again looking like someone had taken that smile away."

Adrian stared at the table.

"It was complicated."

"Love usually is."

"I couldn't give her what she wanted."

"Did she ask you to stop being a soldier?"

"No."

"Did she ask you to choose between her and your duty?"

"No."

Margaret leaned forward.

"Then perhaps you were the one who decided she deserved nothing rather than the little you were afraid to offer."

The words struck harder than Adrian expected.

He pushed his chair back.

"I need some air."

His mother did not stop him.

Outside, the rain had eased to a fine mist. Adrian stood beside the garden wall, breathing in the scent of wet earth and flowering shrubs.

He remembered Elena laughing in this very garden the first time he had brought her home. She had helped his mother plant flowers, getting soil on her trousers and insisting that she knew exactly what she was doing.

By the end of the afternoon, she had managed to plant half the flowers upside down.

Adrian had teased her until she chased him around the garden with a gardening glove.

Later, when his mother was inside preparing dinner, Elena had taken his hand.

"I like it here," she had told him.

"In my mother's garden?"

"With you."

He had kissed her then, slowly, beneath the purple flowers. She had rested her forehead against his and smiled as though the world had finally become simple.

He had wanted to tell her he loved her.

The words had risen to his lips and died there.

He had always believed that loving someone meant giving them something solid to stand on. A future. A promise. A certainty that he would be there when they needed him.

The military had taught him that certainty was an illusion.

So he had given Elena silence instead.

And in doing so, he had lost her anyway.

* * *

Three days after his return, Adrian went to the military hospital for a routine medical assessment.

He arrived early, hoping to finish quickly and spend the rest of the morning at home. His appointment was listed for nine o'clock, but by eight forty-five he was already sitting in a waiting room filled with service members and civilians.

A television played silently in the corner. Someone coughed. A little boy in a blue jacket ran between two chairs until his father caught him by the hand.

Adrian studied the exit.

He had nearly decided to leave when he heard a familiar voice.

"Mr. Kamau, I know you're worried, but your daughter is receiving the care she needs. The specialist will speak with you as soon as the examination is finished."

Adrian turned so quickly that the muscles in his neck tightened.

Elena stood near the reception desk, holding a folder against her chest. Her dark hair was gathered into a loose bun, and a few strands had escaped around her face. She wore a pale blue blouse beneath a charcoal cardigan and carried herself with the calm confidence he remembered.

For several seconds, he forgot how to breathe.

She had changed.

Not in any way he could easily describe. Her face was familiar, her movements were familiar, and the little crease between her eyebrows appeared whenever she was concentrating.

But something about her seemed more settled. Stronger. As if the woman who had once waited for his calls had learned to build a life that did not depend on them.

He should have been happy to see her.

Instead, he felt the terrible weight of everything he had left unsaid.

Elena finished speaking to the worried father and turned toward the corridor.

Her eyes met Adrian's.

The folder slipped slightly in her hands.

Neither of them moved.

Around them, the hospital continued its ordinary rhythm. Phones rang. Nurses passed between departments. A trolley rattled across the floor.

But for Elena, the room became very small.

He was standing only a few metres away, wearing his dark military uniform. His shoulders were broader than she remembered, and his face was leaner. There was a small scar near his left eyebrow that she did not recognize.

He looked tired.

He also looked exactly like the man she had spent months trying not to miss.

"Elena," he said.

One word.

Eleven months of silence, compressed into one word.

She straightened her shoulders.

"Captain Blackwood."

The formal title landed between them like a locked door.

Adrian swallowed.

"It's good to see you."

"Is it?"

Her voice was calm. That made it worse.

He looked at the folder in her hands, then back at her.

"How have you been?"

Elena gave a small, humorless smile.

"I've been living."

He had no answer.

She glanced at the clock on the wall.

"I have work to do."

"Elena, please."

The word stopped her.

She looked at him again, and for a moment he saw the woman who had once reached for his hand without hesitation.

Then the moment passed.

"You don't get to say please as if nothing happened, Adrian."

It was the first time she had used his name.

He felt it somewhere beneath his ribs.

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yes."

"Because I spent months wondering whether you were alive. I wondered whether you were hurt, whether you were coming home, whether I had done something wrong. I wondered if you had decided that I was no longer worth the trouble."

Her voice trembled on the final sentence, but she did not look away.

"And then I got tired of wondering."

Adrian's hands curled at his sides.

He wanted to explain the restrictions, the deployments, the nights when he had thought of her but could not send a message. He wanted to tell her about the fear that followed him everywhere and the way his mind had begun to divide the world into things he could control and things he could not.

But none of those things changed what she had experienced.

He had been absent.

She had been alone.

"I'm sorry," he said.

Elena blinked.

Perhaps she had expected an excuse. Perhaps she had prepared herself for him to insist that his duty had been more important than anything else.

She had not prepared herself for a simple apology.

"I don't know what to do with that," she whispered.

"You don't have to do anything."

"Good."

She stepped around him.

Adrian let her go.

He had learned to follow orders. He had learned to lead men into danger and bring them home. He had learned to make decisions when hesitation could cost lives.

Yet he could not find the courage to ask the woman he loved to stay for another minute.

* * *

Elena made it halfway down the corridor before her eyes began to burn.

She entered an empty records room, closed the door, and leaned against it.

"No," she whispered.

She pressed her fingertips against her eyelids.

"Absolutely not."

She would not cry over him in a hospital storeroom. She would not let eleven months of carefully rebuilt peace collapse because a man had walked into a room wearing a uniform.

She had survived the waiting.

She had survived the unanswered messages.

She had survived the day she finally deleted his number from her phone, only to realize she could still remember every digit.

She could survive seeing him again.

A knock sounded at the door.

"Elena?"

Sophie.

Elena wiped her face quickly and opened the door.

Sophie's expression changed the moment she saw her.

"Oh, sweetheart."

"Don't."

"I'm not going to say anything."

"Your face is saying something."

Sophie pulled her into a hug.

Elena stood stiffly for two seconds before giving in, pressing her forehead against her friend's shoulder.

"I hate that he can still do this to me," she whispered.

Sophie rubbed her back.

"You loved him."

"I know."

"That doesn't disappear just because someone disappoints you."

Elena pulled away.

"I'm not going back to what we were."

"Then don't."

"I'm serious, Sophie."

"I know you are."

Sophie handed her a tissue.

"You don't have to decide anything today. You can be angry. You can be sad. You can even miss him. None of those things mean you have to let him back into your life."

Elena nodded slowly.

Then her phone vibrated.

An unfamiliar number appeared on the screen.

The message was short.

"It's Adrian. I know I have no right to ask for your time. I would still be grateful for one conversation, whenever you're ready. If you're not, I will respect that."

Elena read it once.

Then again.

She could almost hear his voice saying the words. Careful. Formal. As if he were submitting a request that might be denied.

She locked the phone.

"What is it?" Sophie asked.

"Nothing."

But she did not delete the message.

* * *

That night, Adrian sat alone in his apartment with the lights off.

His travel bag remained unpacked beside the bedroom door. A cup of untouched tea sat on the table. Rain tapped against the windows, and somewhere in the building a television played a comedy show that made its audience laugh at regular intervals.

Adrian had never felt so far from another human being.

He took out his phone.

Elena had not replied.

He set it down, stood, walked to the kitchen, and returned to the sofa. He tried reading a book. He managed three pages before realizing he had not understood a single sentence.

Finally, he opened the small notebook he carried with him on deployments.

It was an old habit. When the nights became difficult, he wrote down things he could not say aloud. Names. Memories. Promises he was afraid he would forget.

Near the back of the notebook was a page bearing a single name.

Daniel Hayes.

Beneath it, Adrian had written a sentence months earlier:

I told him I would bring everyone home.

He stared at the words until the room blurred.

Daniel had been twenty-four, a quiet young soldier who had talked endlessly about the woman he intended to marry. He had shown Adrian photographs of the ring he planned to buy. He had complained about the price of apartments. He had joked that he was going to become the sort of husband who cooked dinner every night.

During their final operation together, Daniel had been wounded while helping another soldier reach cover. Adrian had reached him, pressed his hands against the wound, and called for medical support.

Daniel had gripped his sleeve.

"Captain."

"Stay with me."

"Tell Sophie..."

Adrian remembered the confusion of the moment. The explosions. The shouting. The terrible knowledge that Daniel's breathing was becoming weaker.

"Tell her what?"

Daniel had tried to smile.

"Tell her I was coming home."

Adrian had promised.

Daniel had died before the evacuation helicopter arrived.

Since that day, Adrian had carried an invisible weight that no medal could lighten. He had begun to believe that every promise was a potential betrayal, every attachment a weakness someone else might pay for.

Loving Elena had become frightening in a way he could never explain to her.

He had imagined receiving a call that would tell him she was gone. He had imagined returning to find that the world had moved forward without him. He had imagined a hundred disasters, each one so vivid that he had begun to treat the loss of her as if it had already happened.

So he had withdrawn first.

He had called it protection.

Now he understood that fear had made the decision for him, and Elena had paid the price.

His phone vibrated on the table.

Adrian snatched it up.

A reply.

"Tomorrow. Six o'clock. The café near the old railway station. One conversation, Adrian. I am not promising anything else."

He read the message three times.

Then he typed, "Thank you. I'll be there."

He did not add a heart. He did not write that he missed her. He did not tell her that he had never stopped loving her.

He simply sent the message.

And for the first time in eleven months, Adrian Blackwood felt afraid of tomorrow for a reason that had nothing to do with war.

* * *

At six o'clock the following evening, Elena arrived at the café three minutes early.

She had changed her clothes twice before leaving home. First, she had chosen a black dress, then decided it looked as though she had dressed for a funeral. She changed into jeans and a cream blouse, then worried that she looked too casual.

Eventually, she had settled on a simple dark-green dress and a pair of comfortable shoes.

She was not dressing for Adrian.

She repeated that to herself while walking through the café door.

He was already there.

Adrian stood beside a table near the window, wearing a charcoal shirt with the sleeves rolled to his forearms. Without his uniform, he looked strangely unfamiliar. Younger, perhaps. Less like the officer whose name appeared on military documents and more like the man who had once held her hand while they walked through the city.

When he saw her, he stood immediately.

"Elena."

"Adrian."

He pulled out a chair, then seemed to realize that the gesture might feel too familiar.

"Would you like to sit?"

"I can manage a chair, thank you."

A faint, almost helpless smile touched his mouth.

"Right."

She sat.

For a few moments, neither of them spoke. The café was warm and softly lit. A couple near the counter shared a slice of cake. An old man read a newspaper beside the window. The smell of coffee hung in the air.

It was a place where people came to talk.

Adrian had spent months learning how to remain silent.

"I don't expect you to forgive me," he began.

Elena folded her hands on the table.

"Good."

He nodded.

"I deserved that."

"You don't have to keep telling me what you deserve. I am not here to punish you."

"Then why did you come?"

The question seemed to surprise them both.

Elena looked out of the window.

"Because I want to know why."

Adrian waited.

"Why you stopped calling. Why you stopped explaining. Why you let me believe that I had somehow become unimportant to you."

She turned back to him.

"I know you had a difficult deployment. I know there are things about your work that you cannot tell me. I never expected you to describe operations or give away information that could put anyone at risk."

Her voice grew quieter.

"I just needed to know that I still mattered."

Adrian stared at his hands.

"You mattered more than I knew how to handle."

Elena's expression tightened.

"That sounds beautiful, Adrian. But it doesn't explain why you left me alone."

"You're right."

He took a breath.

"I lost someone under my command. His name was Daniel. He was young. He had plans. He was going to get married when we came home."

Elena listened without interrupting.

"I promised him I would get everyone home. I couldn't save him, and after that I started thinking about all the people I could lose if I allowed myself to care about them."

He looked up.

"I was afraid that one day you would be the person waiting for a phone call. I thought if I kept you at a distance, I could spare you that pain."

Elena stared at him.

"So you decided to hurt me yourself?"

The words were not loud.

They did not need to be.

Adrian closed his eyes briefly.

"Yes."

"Without asking me what I wanted?"

"Yes."

"Without giving me the chance to decide whether I was willing to live with the risks?"

"Yes."

Elena looked away.

A tear escaped before she could stop it. She wiped it away quickly, angry at herself for allowing it to fall.

"Do you know what the worst part was?"

Adrian shook his head.

"I would have stayed. If you had told me the truth, I would have stayed. Maybe I would have been frightened. Maybe I would have cried every time you left. Maybe I would have hated the distance and the uncertainty."

She pressed her lips together.

"But I would have stayed because it would have been my choice."

Adrian's eyes shone in the café light.

"I know that now."

"Do you love me?"

The question came so suddenly that he forgot the words he had prepared.

For years, he had imagined saying those three words to Elena. He had imagined saying them in a garden, on a beach, in the quiet of her apartment after a long day. He had imagined a hundred moments in which the words would arrive perfectly, without fear or hesitation.

But love had never waited for a perfect moment.

It had been there when she laughed at his terrible jokes. It had been there when she fell asleep against his shoulder during a film. It had been there when he listened to her describe the future she wanted, and found himself imagining a place for himself inside it.

It had been there when he left.

"Yes," he said.

Elena's breath caught.

"I love you. I have loved you for a long time. I was too afraid to admit what that meant, and I made you pay for my fear."

She looked at him for a long time.

"I loved you too."

The past tense was deliberate.

Adrian heard it.

He did not ask her to change it.

"I don't know if I can go back to who I was with you," Elena continued. "I don't know if I can trust you the way I did before."

"I'm not asking you to."

"And I won't spend another year waiting for someone who refuses to let me know where I stand."

"You shouldn't have to."

She looked down at her hands.

"I need time."

Adrian nodded.

"Take it."

"And I need honesty. Even when it's uncomfortable."

"You'll have it."

Elena studied him, searching for the familiar signs of retreat: the tightening jaw, the guarded expression, the instinctive movement toward silence.

She saw them.

But this time, he did not turn away.

"I can't promise you that I'll never be afraid," he said. "I can't promise that my work will become easy or that the danger will disappear. But I can promise not to make your decisions for you. I can promise to tell you when I am struggling instead of disappearing."

His voice became rough.

"And I can promise that if you decide you don't want me in your life, I won't pretend that I have a right to change your mind."

Elena looked at him for a moment longer.

Then she stood.

Adrian rose with her.

"Thank you for meeting me," he said.

She nodded.

"Goodbye, Adrian."

He wanted to ask whether she meant goodbye forever. He wanted to reach for her hand. He wanted to tell her that he would spend the rest of his life making up for the months he had stolen from them.

Instead, he let her walk toward the door.

She had asked for honesty.

He had to begin by respecting her choice.

Elena stepped into the evening air and drew a long breath. The city moved around her, headlights shining on the wet road as people hurried home.

She had expected to feel victorious after finally confronting him.

Instead, she felt as if she had opened a door she had spent eleven months trying to keep closed.

Behind her, Adrian remained beside the café window.

Neither of them knew what would happen next.

Neither of them knew that the following morning, a message from military headquarters would arrive and change everything.

Neither of them knew that Adrian's return home was about to become far more complicated than a reunion with the woman he loved.

Because war had followed him home.

And this time, the danger would not announce itself with gunfire.
