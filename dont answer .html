Don't Answer the Door at 3:03 AM

The landlord had one rule. Unfortunately, Malik was the kind of man who thought rules were suggestions written by boring people.

Chapter One: The Landlord's Warning

Malik rented the cheapest room in town, which was impressive because the house looked as though it had been assembled by ghosts who had never seen a building before.

The roof leaked. The staircase complained. A portrait of an angry old woman watched everyone from the hallway.

The landlord, Mr. Dube, handed Malik a key.

"Three rules," he said. "Don't open the basement door. Don't whistle after midnight. And if someone knocks at exactly 3:03 in the morning, do not answer."

Malik nodded seriously. "What if it's food delivery?"

"Especially if it's food delivery."

That night, Malik ordered chips. At 3:03 a.m., three knocks sounded from his door.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

"Your chips have arrived," whispered a voice.

Malik sat upright. "Excellent customer service."

He walked to the door.

His phone rang. It was his best friend, Timo, calling from another town.

"Whatever you do," Timo said, "don't open the door."

Malik froze. "How do you know someone's here?"

"Because I stayed in that house last year. The thing outside knows what you want most."

The voice at the door changed. It sounded like Malik's mother.

"My son, open the door. I've brought you food."

Malik's smile disappeared. His mother lived hundreds of kilometres away.

"Mum?" he whispered.

"Open the door, baby."

His hand reached for the lock.

Then another voice came from the hallway.

"Don't listen to it," said the angry woman from the portrait.

Malik turned around. The portrait was empty.

He screamed so loudly that the thing outside began laughing.

Chapter Two: The Thing That Wanted Snacks

Malik barricaded the door with a chair, a suitcase, and a bag of laundry that smelled like a crime scene. He called Timo back.

"You said the thing knows what I want most. What does it want?"

"To get inside."

"No, I mean what does it actually want? Money? Blood? My soul?"

"Nobody knows."

The knocking stopped.

Then came a quiet sniffing sound beneath the door.

"Malik," whispered the voice of his mother, "I know you're hungry."

He looked at the empty chips packet beside his bed.

"This is psychological warfare," he muttered.

A folded paper slid beneath the door. It read: PLEASE LET ME IN. I'M COLD.

Malik wrote back: PUT ON A JACKET.

Something on the other side went silent.

Then it knocked harder.

The hallway light began flickering. The empty portrait rattled against the wall, and a voice from the basement called Malik by his childhood nickname—a name he had never told anyone in that house.

He remembered Mr. Dube's first rule.

Don't open the basement door.

Unfortunately, the basement door began opening by itself.

From the darkness came a tiny creature with long arms, enormous eyes, and a nightcap on its head. It carried a plate of chips.

"Are you the monster?" Malik asked.

"No," the creature said. "I'm the night caretaker."

"You look like a potato that has seen war."

The creature sighed. "I get that a lot."

It explained that the thing outside was a door-hunter, a creature that could imitate anyone a person loved. Once invited inside, it could copy the resident's appearance and steal their place in the world.

"How do we stop it?" Malik asked.

"Don't invite it in."

"I've managed that part."

"And don't eat the chips it offers."

Malik looked at the plate.

"You could have started with that."

Chapter Three: The Worst Night Ever

The door-hunter began imitating Malik himself.

"Open up," the voice outside said. "I'm the real Malik."

The real Malik stared at the door. "If you're me, what did I get on my mathematics exam?"

"An A."

"Liar. I got a C-minus."

The creature outside shrieked and slammed its fists against the wood.

The night caretaker told Malik to ring the old brass bell hanging in the hallway. The bell had been blessed by the first owner of the house, a woman who had trapped the door-hunter decades earlier.

Unfortunately, the bell was beside the front door.

Malik crawled across the floor, avoiding the shadows moving beneath the wallpaper. He reached the bell and pulled the rope.

The sound was small and pathetic.

He pulled harder.

DING.

The door-hunter screamed. Its fake voice shattered into dozens of voices—his mother, Timo, his old teacher, even the voice of a man who had once owed him money.

"You still owe me!" the monster shrieked.

"Even demons are chasing my finances!" Malik shouted.

The bell rang again. The hallway filled with golden light, and the creature outside was dragged backward into the darkness. The front door slammed shut.

Silence returned.

Malik collapsed on the floor, shaking.

Mr. Dube appeared at the top of the stairs wearing slippers and holding a cup of tea.

"Did you answer the door?"

"No."

"Good."

"Your house has a demon, a talking portrait, and a potato caretaker."

"The caretaker prefers 'goblin.'"

Malik stared at him. "I'm moving."

Mr. Dube nodded. "Fair enough. Rent is due tomorrow."

Malik moved out the next morning. He never rented another room without checking the basement, the landlord's references, and whether the house had any portraits.

He also stopped ordering food after midnight.

Mostly.

Three months later, at 3:03 a.m., three knocks sounded on his new apartment door.

Malik sat up, grabbed his phone, and called Timo.

"It's back," he whispered.

From the hallway came a familiar voice.

"Your chips have arrived."

Malik stared at the door.

Then he shouted, "I DIDN'T ORDER ANY!"

A pause.

"Would you like to order some?" the voice asked politely.

Malik moved to a different country the following week.

The demon never found him there.

At least, not until the night he ordered pizza.
