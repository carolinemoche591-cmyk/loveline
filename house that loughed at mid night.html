The House That Laughed at Midnight

The house was cheap, the neighbourhood was quiet, and the landlord promised there were no problems. He forgot to mention that the house thought everything was funny.

Chapter One: A Very Strange Bargain

When Peter found the house, he thought his luck had finally changed.

He had lost his job, his savings were disappearing, and his previous landlord had evicted him after three months of late rent. The new house was enormous, beautiful, and suspiciously cheap.

The owner, an elderly woman named Mrs. Kato, handed him the keys.

"You may use every room except the one at the end of the upstairs corridor," she said.

"What's in there?"

"Memories."

Peter waited for her to explain.

She did not.

He moved in on Saturday. By Sunday evening, he had decided that the house was perfect. It had a large kitchen, a comfortable bedroom, and a garden full of flowers. The only strange thing was that the walls occasionally made a sound like someone trying not to laugh.

At 11:58 p.m., Peter heard a giggle from the hallway.

He opened his bedroom door.

The corridor was empty.

"Very funny," he said.

The walls erupted in laughter.

Peter slammed the door.

At midnight, something knocked from inside his wardrobe.

"If you're a ghost," Peter called, "please understand that I'm unemployed. There is nothing here worth haunting."

A tiny voice replied, "We know."

Peter stared at the wardrobe.

"Who is we?"

"Everyone."

He packed a bag and tried to leave. The front door opened onto the kitchen. He tried the back door. It led to the upstairs bathroom.

He opened a window and looked outside. The garden was there, but every flower had a tiny human face.

One flower winked at him.

Peter closed the window.

"Fine," he whispered. "I'll sleep here. But if anything eats me, I want it known that I was against this arrangement."

The walls giggled again.

Chapter Two: The Room at the End

By the third day, Peter had learned that the house enjoyed practical jokes. It moved his shoes, replaced his toothpaste with whipped cream, and whispered embarrassing childhood memories while he cooked.

It was irritating, but not entirely terrifying.

Then he discovered the laughter was hiding something.

Every night, the house became silent for exactly one minute. During that minute, Peter could hear a child crying behind the locked room at the end of the corridor.

On Thursday, he asked Mrs. Kato about it.

"What happened in that room?"

She went quiet. "My daughter lived there. Her name was Miriam."

Miriam had died many years earlier in a fire. Mrs. Kato had survived, but the house had changed after the tragedy. It began laughing at strange moments, hiding objects, and playing tricks on anyone who stayed too long.

"Why rent it out?" Peter asked.

"Because the house gets lonely."

"Houses do not get lonely."

Mrs. Kato gave him a sad smile. "Then you should have no trouble leaving."

Peter decided he would open the locked room. He found an old key hidden beneath a loose floorboard. When he turned it in the lock, the laughter stopped.

Inside was a child's bedroom, untouched by time. There were toys on the floor, drawings on the walls, and a small music box beside the bed.

Peter opened the box.

A girl's voice whispered, "You came back."

Every window in the room slammed shut.

The walls began to shake. Peter stumbled backward as a dark shape rose from beneath the bed. It had a child's outline, but its shadow stretched across the ceiling like enormous claws.

"You promised," the voice said.

Peter ran.

He reached the hallway just as the clock struck midnight. The entire house began laughing—not its usual playful giggle, but a deep, furious roar that shook dust from the ceiling.

On the wall, the family portrait changed.

Peter saw Mrs. Kato standing beside a little girl. Behind them was a man whose face had been scratched out.

Below the picture, words appeared in the wallpaper:

HE LEFT US HERE.

Chapter Three: The Last Joke

Peter called Mrs. Kato and demanded the truth. This time, she told him everything.

Years earlier, a fire had started in the locked bedroom. Mrs. Kato's husband had been drinking and had accidentally blocked the upstairs corridor with furniture. He escaped the house alone. Miriam was trapped in her room, and Mrs. Kato was unable to reach her in time.

Her husband later disappeared. Mrs. Kato remained in the house, drowning in guilt. She believed her daughter had become part of the building because Miriam had loved playing hide-and-seek there.

"The house isn't trying to kill you," she said through the phone. "It wants someone to find what happened."

Peter returned to the locked room. The shadow waited beside the bed.

"I didn't leave you," he said, not knowing whether he was speaking to the child or the house itself. "But I can help you tell your story."

He searched the room and found a small metal box hidden behind a drawer. Inside were letters from Miriam's father, including one in which he admitted that he had locked the corridor door during an argument and forgotten to unlock it before the fire.

Peter brought the letters to Mrs. Kato. She wept as she read them. For years, she had blamed herself without knowing the full truth. The letters did not undo the tragedy, but they ended the silence surrounding it.

That night, Peter placed the music box beside the bedroom window and played its tune.

"I'm sorry nobody listened," he whispered.

The house became still.

Then came a small giggle.

Not the mocking laughter that had frightened him, but the sound of a child enjoying a private joke.

The dark shape appeared one final time. It looked less frightening now, more like a little girl wearing a nightdress.

"Thank you," she said.

The shadow faded into the morning light.

Peter woke the next day to find every door working normally. The flowers outside had returned to ordinary flowers. The portrait no longer changed. The room at the end of the corridor was empty except for the music box.

Mrs. Kato gave Peter his rent back and told him he could stay if he wished.

"Is the house safe now?" he asked.

"I think so."

"Will the walls stop laughing?"

She considered this. "Probably."

Peter moved out anyway. He had learned that affordable housing came at a price, and he preferred neighbours who complained about loud music rather than supernatural activity.

Months later, he visited Mrs. Kato to check on her. The house looked peaceful, and she seemed lighter than before.

As Peter reached the gate, he heard a familiar giggle from the upstairs window.

He froze.

A small voice called, "Peter!"

He slowly turned.

"Yes?"

"You forgot your shoes!"

Peter looked down. He was wearing his shoes.

Then he remembered the house had always loved jokes.

He laughed, shook his head, and walked away.

Behind him, the house gave one final, happy chuckle.

And for the first time in many years, midnight arrived without anyone crying.
